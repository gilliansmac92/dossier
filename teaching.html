Teaching Philosophy

When I began teaching history in 2015, I was most concerned with getting students to engage with me instead of sitting in a silent room. I remember a fellow teachign assistant asking me asked me: “how are you going to get students to talk?” Thinking back now, getting them to talk wasn’t the goal. I have, however, returned to this assertion that engagement fosters learning but it has to be useful. Subsequently, I have focused on creating an interactive and inclusive learning environment that encourages critical thinking and active participation. For me, this means teaching with intentionality and designing courses, assignments, and activities where students from different technical backgrounds, learning styles, and career goals can succeed and find meaningful connection to the material. To that end, I use diverse historical sources that center multiple perspectives to challenge students to see whose stories have been preserved and whose have been lost in historical archives. I design assignments with flexibility so students can pursue questions that matter to them and still meeting learning objectives. When teaching digital methods, I create low-stakes environments for experimentation where students feel comfortable trying new tools without fear of failure. I work deliberately to make both history and digital methods exciting and relevant to students who might not initially see themselves as in the field.


Whether I’m teaching content-based or methodology-based courses my assignments mirror authentic research and professional work and center on how we use sources to construct narratives and arguments. Sources are not transparent windows but carefully constructed narratives that require critical interpretation. And whether students are working with seventeenth-century correspondence, nineteenth-century newspapers, or digital archives, they learn to ask: Who created this source? For what purpose? What does it reveal and what does it leave out? How do we transform disparate sources into coherent historical narratives? Students learn information literacy skills including how to evaluate evidence, construct arguments from incomplete information, and understand the politics of documentation and preservation, which are directly applicable to contemporary misinformation challenges. In my digital methods class, I introduce the toolkit first—helping students gain comfort and competency with digital methods—and then guide them to apply these tools to historical questions. One of the exercises I engage students in is bias detection with digital archives and generative artificial intelligence; here they get to explore what information counts and matters and how that gets translated to statistical models and their training data. Once students feel confident with the tools, they can engage more deeply with the intellectual questions those tools help us explore.


Ultimately, my goal as an educator is to empower students to become critical thinkers, effective communicators, and engaged citizens. Being friendly and engaging does not equal learning, but it does help produce an environment wherein students can take control of their own education and feel comfortable sharing ideas. This was especially true of teaching during the pandemic with new modalities like HyFlex, where both the students and I participated in a somewhat unnerving experiment that exacerbated inequalities for some. I do not want students or myself just to regurgitate textbooks, but, more importantly, to be able to think for themselves. At a more fundamental level, my goal is to help my students gain digital and epistemic literacy. In an age where misinformation is rampant, I believe it is crucial for students to develop the skills to critically evaluate sources, understand the construction of knowledge, and navigate the digital landscape effectively. By fostering these skills, I aim to prepare my students not only for academic success but also for informed and responsible participation in society.


Curriculum Development

As part of my role as Director of LEADR, I play an integral role in the ongoing redesign of the history major at MSU, which includes proposals for internship options and courses featuring new web-based projects. This is driven by the lab's mission to enhance traditional liberal arts education by integrating modern digital technologies and methodologies. This approach aligns with the broader institutional commitment at Michigan State University to equip students with the knowledge, attitudes, and skills necessary for 21st-century leadership and lifelong learning.

Core Principles:

  • Bridging Traditional and Digital Competencies: LEADR’s curriculum focuses on bridging the gap between traditional skills—such as field knowledge, critical thinking, research, and writing—and modern digital practices. This is achieved by offering seamless integration of digital projects and assignments into courses across History and Anthropology.
  • Fostering Analytical Thinking and Digital Fluency: The curriculum emphasizes hands-on learning, encouraging students to step outside their comfort zones. Students engage in practical skills like data mining, network visualization, digital mapping, and web-based storytelling. This experience directly reinforces MSU's Undergraduate Learning Goals for Analytical Thinking, enabling students to acquire, analyze, and evaluate information from multiple sources using quantitative and humanistic methods.
  • Enhancing Communication and Public Engagement: A central element of the curriculum is the development of public-facing projects. By making student work available to wider audiences, LEADR meets MSU's goal for effective communication using a variety of media. This approach fosters pride in students' work and ensures they can effectively communicate complex material to diverse audiences.
  • Promoting Experiential and Collaborative Learning: The lab utilizes research-engaged active learning (REAL) principles and supports methods like flipped teaching. The curriculum is built on collaboration, where students work with peers, faculty, and digital technology specialists to create innovative projects, fostering a supportive community of digital practitioners.

LEADR provides a critical pedagogical model, focusing on the practical application of interdisciplinary knowledge through digital tools, thereby preparing MSU graduates not only with historical content mastery but also with the essential 21st-century skills needed to thrive in a rapidly evolving digital landscape. The core of my role as a mentor in this environment involves training graduate students in digital pedagogy. Their training goes beyond simply learning tools—they’ll develop pedagogical frameworks for making technical skills accessible to diverse learners, build digital literacy progressively, and connect digital methods to disciplinary questions and critical thinking. We discuss how to troubleshoot technical problems in real-time, how to assess digital projects meaningfully, how to create inclusive learning environments, and increasingly, how to teach students to use emerging technologies like generative AI responsibly and effectively. This work demonstrates our commitment to addressing contemporary challenges in digital pedagogy.

LEADR is also the home of the Cultural Heritage Informatics fellowship run in partnership with the Anthropology Department at MSU. The fellowship is highly competitive and has a successful 15-year history where fellows have gone on to get highly competitive jobs with their exceptional digital skills. What makes this model successful is that we're not asking students to choose between traditional disciplinary expertise and computational approaches—we're showing them how these enhance each other. A history student might use network analysis to trace intellectual networks while still doing deep archival work while an anthropology student might use GIS for spatial analysis while maintaining ethnographic rigor. Each fellow completes a tangible project—a digital edition, a data visualization, a public-facing resource—that becomes part of their professional portfolio. This prepares them for academic jobs where digital skills are increasingly expected, and also for alt-ac careers if that's their path. The fellowship is a rigourous and applied experience which teaches graduate students fundamental computational skills and their application to cultural heritage.

Course Development

My experience designing several courses from scratch has given me a systematic understanding of how to build coherent learning experiences. I approach course design by starting with learning outcomes, then working backward to design assessments and activities that help students achieve those outcomes. When designing new courses, I think carefully about scaffolding—how early assignments build skills and knowledge that students will need for more complex later work. I also consider how to create courses that serve diverse student populations.


For example, when I designed "Doing Digital History" (HST 251) at MSU, I started by identifying key digital skills and historical methods I wanted students to learn, such as data archaeology, data visualization, digital literacy, and digital storytelling. I then created assignments that progressively built these skills, starting with low-stakes exercises and culminating in a final project where students created their own digital history project. I incorporated a mix of lectures, hands-on workshops, and collaborative projects to cater to different learning styles. I also ensured that the course content was inclusive and representative of diverse perspectives in history. Later in the semester, when the students were more confident with the tools, we engaged with the conversation on generative artificial intelligence. This involved several different low-stakes exercises including: bias detection (they prompted ChatGPT or Claude to give them different perspectives on an event and analyzed them) this gave students a window into training data, we also asked the chatbots to analyze sources (the students then corrected the chatbots when they were off course), and finally we had some fun and engaged in an exercise in rapid development of very small websites which we then put on GitHub. This approach came to fruition in my student evaluations for the class. Students appreciated the hands-on learning and the opportunity to engage critically with emerging technologies. One student commented that because of the way the class was structured, they learned that failure isn't always a bad thing and now they could think in different ways and analyze deeper than before.


In another example, when I developed "War and Revolution: Espionage" (ISS 325) I focused on creating a course that combined historical content, digital skills, and critical analysis of primary sources. I designed assignments that encouraged students to analyze espionage tactics from multiple perspectives, including political, social, and ethical dimensions. The course included a mix of traditional lectures, group discussions, source analysis, and digital visualization of otherwise hidden infrastructures of espionage. This fostered critical thinking and engagement with the material. By the end of the course, students had followed and traced the evolution of espionage from the ancient world through to the 21st century. In their final projects, students worked in groups and created podcasts tracing important events or creating biographical sketches of spies they found interesting.


Overall, my course development experience has taught me the importance of intentional design, scaffolding, inclusivity, and adaptability in creating effective learning experiences. From teaching to research, to mentoring and back, my teaching is always evolving to meet new needs and benchmarks. I continually work to improve my teaching through student feedback, professional development workshops, pedagogy training, and experimentation with new methods. During a particularly difficult semester at MSU (after the shooting in February of 2023) I was nominated for a teaching award because of the way I handled returning to the classroom in the aftermath.
