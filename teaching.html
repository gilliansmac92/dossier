Teaching Philosophy

When I began teaching history in 2015, I was most concerned with getting students to engage with me instead of sitting in a silent room. I remember a fellow teachign assistant asking me asked me: “how are you going to get students to talk?” Thinking back now, getting them to talk wasn’t the goal. I have, however, returned to this assertion that engagement fosters learning but it has to be useful. Subsequently, I have focused on creating an interactive and inclusive learning environment that encourages critical thinking and active participation.


For me, this means teaching with intentionality and designing courses, assignments, and activities where students from different technical backgrounds, learning styles, and career goals can succeed and find meaningful connection to the material. To that end, I use diverse historical sources that center multiple perspectives to challenge students to see whose stories have been preserved and whose have been lost in historical archives. I design assignments with flexibility so students can pursue questions that matter to them and still meeting learning objectives. When teaching digital methods, I create low-stakes environments for experimentation where students feel comfortable trying new tools without fear of failure. I work deliberately to make both history and digital methods exciting and relevant to students who might not initially see themselves as in the field.


Ultimately, my goal as an educator is to empower students to become critical thinkers, effective communicators, and engaged citizens. Being friendly and engaging does not equal learning, but it does help produce an environment wherein students can take control of their own education and feel comfortable sharing ideas. This was especially true of teaching during the pandemic with new modalities like HyFlex, where both the students and I participated in a somewhat unnerving experiment that exacerbated inequalities for some. I do not want students or myself just to regurgitate textbooks, but, more importantly, to be able to think for themselves. At a more fundamental level, my goal is to help my students gain digital and epistemic literacy. In an age where misinformation is rampant, I believe it is crucial for students to develop the skills to critically evaluate sources, understand the construction of knowledge, and navigate the digital landscape effectively. By fostering these skills, I aim to prepare my students not only for academic success but also for informed and responsible participation in society.


Whether I’m teaching content-based or methodology-based courses my assignments mirror authentic research and professional work and center on how we use sources to construct narratives and arguments. Sources are not transparent windows but carefully constructed narratives that require critical interpretation. And whether students are working with seventeenth-century correspondence, nineteenth-century newspapers, or digital archives, they learn to ask: Who created this source? For what purpose? What does it reveal and what does it leave out? How do we transform disparate sources into coherent historical narratives? Students learn information literacy skills including how to evaluate evidence, construct arguments from incomplete information, and understand the politics of documentation and preservation, which are directly applicable to contemporary misinformation challenges. In my digital methods class, I introduce the toolkit first—helping students gain comfort and competency with digital methods—and then guide them to apply these tools to historical questions. One of the exercises I engage students in is bias detection with digital archives and generative artificial intelligence; here they get to explore what information counts and matters and how that gets translated to statistical models and their training data. Once students feel confident with the tools, they can engage more deeply with the intellectual questions those tools help us explore.
