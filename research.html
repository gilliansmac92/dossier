Research

My research trajectory and output takes several forms. I am engaged in digital scholarship, text-based scholarship, and public scholarship. My own research is focused on the seventeenth and eighteenth century revolutionary period particularly in the Atlantic world, my recent research has been focused on networks of information and people throughout the burgeoning British empire.

My scholarship is arranged by project rather than by type, this means that all of my scholarship--text-based or not--is present within this public dossier. It is a more holistic version of my output as a researcher. My current research investigates how information networks have shaped political and cultural life from the early modern period to the present. Currently, I am digitally reconstructing seventeenth-century those correspondence networks in Networking the Revolution, this combines traditional methods with digital techniques, allowing me to trace patterns of information exchange that would otherwise remain invisible. While the project is focused on 1688 to 1692 respectively, it unpacks the importance of stable and viable communication networks writ large during the late seventeenth-century and early eighteenth century between Scotland, England, Ireland, and the Atlantic world. I work closely with an undergraduate colleague whose expertise in data science has been indispensable to the project. We examine in detail historical practices of collecting, circulating, and organizing knowledge and how those practices shaped early modern political and cultural life.


Another strand of my research focuses on the history of border policies and migration in the British Atlantic world. My book project, Challenging Borders, explores how border policies were influenced by the revolutionary chaos of the late-seventeenth century and the development of the British Empire and its colonies. By analyzing government documents, personal narratives, correspondence patterns, and trade data, I uncover the ways in which border policies shaped social hierarchies, economic practices, and cultural identities across the Atlantic world. This work contributes to a deeper understanding of the complexities of empire-building and the lived experiences of many people during this period.

In addition to my research on early modern information networks, I am also engaged in public scholarship that brings historical methods and insights to contemporary audiences. My work as Director of the Lab for the Education and Advancement in Digital Research (LEADR) focuses on creating digital research, classroom alternatives, and public history projects that engage diverse audiences. These projects include digital exhibitions, interactive maps, and online archives that make historical knowledge accessible and relevant to a broad public. Through these initiatives, I aim to demonstrate the value of historical scholarship in understanding present-day issues and fostering informed civic engagement.


I am privileged to be a part of several ongoing collaborative projects including:

  • Digital Mark Twain Day by Day: imagines a total revision of David Fears’s seminal work, Mark Twain Day by Day. The project would link 27,000+ days of Twain’s life to all his works, places he visited, and people he knew through network analysis and linked open data. It will create an immensely rich resource for not only researchers and students, but the public as well.
  • A Search for Research: stands as a centralized hub for scholars to locate needed archives and documents and serves as a platform to generate conversation and collaboration, including an interactive map that highlights archives and other primary source collections that are related to the histories of Ukraine and Russia but are located outside the region.
  • Radical Historians Newsletter: provides access to a pivotal collection of newsletters that documents historical events and historiography from 1969 to 2002. The digital project stands as a searchable knowledgebase that will also provide insights into the collection and pedagogical materials in the near future.
