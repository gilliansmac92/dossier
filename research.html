Research

My research trajectory and output takes several forms. I am engaged in digital scholarship, text-based scholarship, and public scholarship. My own research is focused on the seventeenth and eighteenth century revolutionary period particularly in the Atlantic world, my recent research has been focused on networks of information and people throughout the burgeoning British empire.

My scholarship is arranged by project rather than by type, this means that all of my scholarship--text-based or not--is present within this public dossier. It is a more holistic version of my output as a researcher. My current research investigates how information networks have shaped political and cultural life from the early modern period to the present. Currently, I am digitally reconstructing seventeenth-century those correspondence networks in Networking the Revolution, this project combines traditional methods with digital techniques, allowing me to trace patterns of information exchange that would otherwise remain invisible. While the project is focused on 1688 to 1692 respectively, it unpacks the importance of stable and viable communication networks writ large during the late seventeenth-century and early eighteenth century between Scotland, England, Ireland, and the Atlantic world. I work closely with an undergraduate colleague whose expertise in data science has been indispensable to the project. We examine in detail historical practices of collecting, circulating, and organizing knowledge and how those practices shaped early modern political and cultural life.


Another strand of my research focuses on the history of border policies and migration in the British Atlantic world. My book project, Challenging Borders, explores how border policies were influenced by the revolutionary chaos of the late-seventeenth century and the development of the British Empire and its colonies. The project treats the Irish Sea as a maritime frontier which shaped much of the Scottish administration’s policy during the late-seventeenth century rebellion. Based on a systematic analysis of approximately 3.25 years of Privy Council Registers, four sessions of legislation, 1500 manuscript exchequer documents, and more than 2000 letters, the depth and frequency of measures reveals the importance of the sea borders and reveals that European power politics, diplomacy, geopolitics, and potential invasions suggest that focusing only on the land war is an inadequate approach. I argue that border policy was fundamentally shaped by people and their networks of information whether at a local or national level. By analyzing these government documents, personal narratives, correspondence patterns, and trade data, I uncover the ways in which border policies shaped social hierarchies, economic practices, and cultural identities across the Atlantic world. This work contributes to a deeper understanding of the complexities of empire-building and the lived experiences of many people during this period.Information whether it was real or imagined informed politics, intelligence and even rumor can change policy.

In addition to my research on early modern information networks, I am also engaged in public scholarship that brings historical methods and insights to contemporary audiences. My work as Director of LEADR focuses on creating digital research, classroom alternatives, and public history projects that engage diverse audiences. These projects include digital exhibitions, interactive maps, and online archives that make historical knowledge accessible and relevant to a broad public. Through these initiatives, I aim to demonstrate the value of historical scholarship in understanding present-day issues and fostering informed civic engagement. Recently LEADR has been engaged in digital literacy, ethics, and generative AI methods in history—work that led to my organizing the "Faith, Fear, and Praxis: AI and the Humanities and Social Sciences" symposium and my appointment to MSU's Provost's Planning Committee for the upcoming AI Summit in May 2026. In part of a bigger collaboration, I plan to design and build a digital literacy and ethics network that will serve as an essentialspace for disciplinary conversations as these technologies rapidly transform research, teaching, and professional practice.


I am privileged to be a part of several ongoing collaborative projects including:

  • Digital Mark Twain Day by Day: imagines a total revision of David Fears’s seminal work, Mark Twain Day by Day. The project would link 27,000+ days of Twain’s life to all his works, places he visited, and people he knew through network analysis and linked open data. It will create an immensely rich resource for not only researchers and students, but the public as well.
  • A Search for Research: stands as a centralized hub for scholars to locate needed archives and documents and serves as a platform to generate conversation and collaboration, including an interactive map that highlights archives and other primary source collections that are related to the histories of Ukraine and Russia but are located outside the region.
  • Radical Historians Newsletter: a digital project that provides access to a pivotal collection of newsletters that documents historical events and historiography from 1969 to 2002. The digital project stands as a searchable knowledgebase that will also provide insights into the collection and pedagogical materials in the near future.
  • ScotHub: is a collaborative project between myself and Dr. Jim Ambuske. It will be an integrated platform to foster greater community, research, and collaboration among Scottish history practitioners and enthusiasts globally. Scot Hub will be built on three key pillars: 1. H-Net’s existing pool of Scottish history monograph reviews from H-Albion and other networks; 2. Curated collections of existing and new digital projects, exhibits, and podcast series centered on Scottish history; and 3. Incorporation of an in-development new podcast/livestream series by Scot Hub’s editors featuring conversations with Scottish history experts.
